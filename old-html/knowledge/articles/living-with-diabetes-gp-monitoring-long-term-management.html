Introduction

Living with diabetes involves more than checking a single blood glucose reading. A practical long-term care plan considers glucose trends, heart and kidney health, medicines, daily routines and the complications that can develop gradually. Your GP can work with you to agree on goals that fit your health and circumstances.

Why This Topic Matters

Diabetes affects blood vessels and nerves over time. Regular review can identify changes early and create opportunities to adjust treatment, address cardiovascular risks and organise appropriate screening. HbA1c reflects average glucose over roughly two to three months, but the right target differs between patients.

Signs, Symptoms or Situations Relevant to Patients

The following situations are worth recognising. They do not establish a diagnosis by themselves:

  • Blood glucose readings repeatedly outside the personalised range agreed with your clinician.
  • Changes in vision, tingling or loss of sensation in the feet, or wounds that heal slowly.
  • Thirst, frequent urination, recurrent infections or unexpected weight change.

When Should I Seek Medical Advice?

Arrange a GP appointment for new symptoms, repeated low or high glucose readings, medicine side effects or missed diabetes reviews. A foot wound, especially with redness or infection, needs prompt assessment. Severe confusion, collapse, vomiting with dehydration or symptoms of a serious glucose emergency require urgent care or 000.

How Can a GP Help?

Your GP can review HbA1c, blood pressure, kidney tests, lipid management, medicine use and individual nutrition or movement goals. Care may also involve retinal examinations, foot assessments, diabetes education and referrals to allied health. Where appropriate, a GP chronic condition management plan can coordinate follow-up under current Medicare rules. Patients from Woolloongabba and nearby Brisbane inner-south communities can discuss the most appropriate next step with the practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should HbA1c be checked?

The interval depends on diabetes type, treatment changes, stability and individual targets. Your GP will advise a suitable schedule rather than using one interval for everyone.

Is a foot check important if my feet feel normal?

Yes. Some nerve and circulation changes have few early symptoms. Your care team can recommend examination and self-care based on your risk.

Can diabetes management include other professionals?

Yes. Depending on need, a diabetes educator, dietitian, podiatrist, optometrist or specialist may contribute to coordinated care.
