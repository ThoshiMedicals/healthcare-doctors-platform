Accreditation in plain language.

General practice accreditation is an independent assessment of the systems a practice uses to support safe and quality care. In Australia, accreditation under the National General Practice Accreditation Scheme assesses practices against the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) Standards for general practices.

It is not an advertising award and it is not a guarantee that every consultation will have a particular outcome. It means the practice has demonstrated that important systems, responsibilities and safeguards are in place and have been independently assessed against national requirements.

Our current status.

Woolloongabba Medical Centre is listed on the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care register as an accredited general practice at 468 Vulture Street, Woolloongabba, with accreditation recorded through 22 March 2029.

Accreditation status checked against the national register on 7 October 2026.

The standard used today.

As at October 2026, the National General Practice Accreditation Scheme continues to assess practices against the RACGP Standards for general practices, 5th edition. The 6th edition has been released and practices are being encouraged to use its guidance as they review and improve their systems.

Patient safety.

Accreditation looks beyond the consultation room. It considers whether a practice has dependable systems to reduce avoidable harm and respond when something does not go as planned.

For patients, this can include safe and well-maintained equipment, appropriately trained staff, reliable patient identification, safe medicine and vaccine handling, emergency arrangements, clear follow-up processes and systems for managing clinical risk.

The purpose is practical: important safety steps should be built into everyday practice rather than depend on memory or individual habit.

Healthcare quality.

Quality in general practice is not only about clinical knowledge. It also depends on how care is organised, communicated and followed through over time.

The RACGP Standards cover areas such as patient-centred communication, informed decisions, respectful care, accessibility, comprehensive care, clinical handover, health records, practitioner qualifications and the safe use of medicines. Accreditation assesses whether the practice has systems that support these expectations consistently.

This helps create a more reliable experience for patients across appointments, practitioners and different stages of care.

Clinical governance.

Clinical governance is the framework that makes clear who is responsible for safety and quality, how risks are managed and how the practice learns from problems.

In patient terms, it means there should be clear responsibilities, appropriate staff training, documented policies, effective teamwork, incident and risk management, emergency planning and oversight of systems that affect care.

Good governance does not remove clinical judgement. It provides the structure around clinical care so that responsibilities are understood and important safety issues are identified, escalated and addressed.

Infection prevention.

General practices need systems that reduce the risk of infection for patients, staff and visitors. Accreditation includes requirements relating to infection prevention and control, including sterilisation and the safe handling of clinical equipment where applicable.

In practice, this can involve hand hygiene, cleaning, reprocessing or sterilising reusable equipment, safe sharps and waste handling, maintaining a suitable clinical environment, and protecting vaccine potency through appropriate storage and temperature control.

These processes are part of routine patient safety, even when they are not visible during an appointment.

Patient privacy.

Health information is highly sensitive. Accreditation standards require practices to have systems for confidentiality, privacy, secure health records and information security.

For patients, this means the practice should control who can access clinical information, protect records from inappropriate access or disclosure, manage conversations and communications with appropriate privacy, and explain how personal health information is handled.

Accreditation works alongside Australian privacy law and professional confidentiality obligations; it does not replace them.

Continuous improvement.

Accreditation is not intended to be a one-time inspection followed by business as usual. The standards require practices to undertake quality-improvement activities and use information, feedback and experience to strengthen care.

This may include reviewing patient feedback and complaints, examining incidents or near misses, auditing clinical data, improving administrative processes, updating policies, strengthening follow-up systems and measuring whether changes have actually helped.

The important principle is that a practice should be able to identify opportunities to improve, act on them and learn from the result.

What accreditation does — and does not — tell you.

It does tell youThe practice has been independently assessed against nationally recognised general practice safety and quality standards.
It does not guaranteeA particular diagnosis, treatment result, waiting time or individual experience at every visit.
It does tell youImportant systems for governance, privacy, infection prevention, risk management and quality improvement are part of the assessment.
It does not replaceYour right to ask questions, give feedback, raise a concern or make a complaint about your care.

How accreditation is assessed.

Practices seeking accreditation work through the applicable standards and provide evidence about their systems and processes. An approved accrediting agency then undertakes an independent assessment. Accreditation is awarded only when the practice meets the requirements of the scheme.

The national scheme is overseen by the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care. AGPAL is one of the accrediting agencies that assesses general practices under that framework.

If requirements are not met, practices may need to provide further evidence or corrective action before accreditation can be confirmed.

Why this matters at Woolloongabba Medical Centre.

For our patients, the value of accreditation is not the certificate on the wall. It is the expectation that the systems behind everyday care are organised around safety, accountability, privacy, infection prevention and ongoing improvement.

Those systems sit alongside the clinical relationship between you and your practitioner. If you are unsure about any aspect of your care, privacy, follow-up or safety, you are encouraged to ask our team.

Independent information.

For a neutral explanation of general practice accreditation, you can read the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care consumer information or check the national register of accredited practices. AGPAL also publishes information for healthcare consumers.
